**Gemmotherapy** is winning over more and more natural wellness enthusiasts, but it is not risk-free for everyone. Before starting a cure, it is worth knowing the real dangers of gemmotherapy, its contraindications, and the precautions to follow depending on individual health profiles.

## What is gemmotherapy?

Gemmotherapy is a branch of herbal medicine that uses the embryonic tissue of plants: buds, young shoots and rootlets, harvested in spring before they open. These tissues concentrate much of the active potential the plant will later develop in its leaves, flowers or bark. For an overview of blackcurrant bud, one of the most emblematic macerates, see our guide on the [benefits of blackcurrant bud gemmotherapy](/en/blog/blackcurrant-bud-gemmotherapy-benefits/).

Buds are macerated in a water-alcohol-glycerin mixture to produce a mother macerate, the most common form sold in pharmacies and specialty stores. The process, developed in the 20th century by homeopathic physicians and later refined by modern herbal medicine, generally takes several weeks and aims to concentrate the active compounds present in the embryonic tissue. This composition is precisely the source of several precautions detailed below.

## Contraindications of gemmotherapy: who should be cautious?

Certain profiles should avoid gemmotherapy or only use it under medical supervision.

### Pregnant and breastfeeding women

As a precaution, most buds are not recommended during pregnancy and breastfeeding. Safety data is lacking for this population, and some macerates (blackcurrant, raspberry) have hormone-like activity that calls for strict caution. Medical advice is required before any intake, regardless of trimester.

### Children: what precautions apply?

For children under 3, gemmotherapy is not recommended without medical advice, due to the lack of specific pediatric data and the presence of alcohol in the classic mother macerate. Beyond age 3, dosage adapted to body weight and advice from a trained professional remain necessary.

## What are the dangers and side effects of buds?

Taken in isolation and at recommended dosages, gemmotherapy rarely causes serious adverse effects. The most documented reactions remain mild: minor digestive upset when taken on an empty stomach, or a skin reaction with topical use. The main danger therefore comes less from the bud itself than from the context of use: self-medication in the case of an undiagnosed condition, interaction with an ongoing treatment, or an excessive dose over a prolonged period.

Prolonged overdosing of certain buds with hormonal or cortisone-like activity can disrupt hormonal or blood pressure balance in an already fragile person. This is why gemmotherapy should never replace an established medical treatment, but rather be considered as a complement, with the treating physician's agreement in the case of a chronic condition.

The risk of allergy, although rare, also exists: as with any plant, individual sensitivity to the bud used or its compounds can appear as itching, redness or mild digestive trouble. It is advisable to start with a minimal dose, especially when trying a bud for the first time, in order to observe tolerance over a few days before reaching the full dosage.

## Alcohol content in macerates: a risk for certain groups?

An often overlooked point: the classic mother macerate contains alcohol, used as a solvent and preservative during maceration. This presence of alcohol, even in small quantities per dose, warrants particular vigilance for certain groups: people recovering from alcohol dependency, people with alcohol-related liver disease, or people on treatment incompatible with alcohol (certain antibiotics, for example).

Alcohol-free alternatives exist from several manufacturers, in the form of pure glycerin macerates. It is recommended to check the exact composition on the label before purchasing, particularly for these sensitive groups, and to favor [the best gemmotherapy brands for beginners](/en/blog/best-gemmotherapy-brands-beginners/) that clearly state their manufacturing process.

## Dosage and precautions to limit risks

Most of the reported dangers around gemmotherapy stem from failing to follow the recommended dosage rather than from any inherent toxicity of the buds.

### Following the correct dose and cure length

The standard adult dosage is between 5 and 15 drops of mother macerate diluted in a little water, 1 to 3 times a day, away from meals. The cure generally lasts 3 weeks, followed by a break before renewal. To understand how long it takes for effects to appear and adjust the length of a cure, see our article on [how long gemmotherapy takes to work](/en/blog/gemmotherapy-how-long-does-it-take-to-work/). Systematically starting at a low dose makes it possible to assess individual tolerance before reaching the full dose.

## Interactions with medical treatments: when to consult a doctor?

Certain buds interact with common treatments. Blackcurrant, for example, can potentiate the effect of anticoagulants and interfere with cortisone treatment due to its action on the adrenal glands. Medical advice is recommended before any cure for people on chronic treatment: anticoagulants, corticosteroids, hormone therapy, or immunosuppressants.

Consulting a doctor or a pharmacist trained in herbal medicine is also advised in case of doubt about an interaction, or before combining several buds in the same cure. As with other natural remedies, [the dangers and contraindications of black seed oil](/en/blog/black-seed-oil-dangers/) are a reminder that even plant-based products can require this kind of precaution.

## Buds to use with caution

Some buds, widely used in gemmotherapy, call for particular vigilance due to their pronounced biological activity.

### Blackcurrant bud: a hormone-like macerate

Blackcurrant bud, one of the most used for its anti-inflammatory action, has cortisone-like activity through stimulation of the adrenal glands. It is not recommended for people with unbalanced high blood pressure and under cortisone treatment without medical advice. Find the full detail of its uses and dosage in our article on [the benefits of blackcurrant bud](/en/blog/blackcurrant-bud-gemmotherapy-benefits/).

### Fig bud and linden bud

Fig bud, often recommended for anxiety and digestive troubles of nervous origin, has no known major contraindication, but should still be avoided in prolonged self-medication without reassessment. Read more on its [uses, benefits, and dosage](/en/blog/fig-bud-uses-benefits/). Linden bud, used for sleep, is generally well tolerated, with the same note of caution for pregnant women and young children.

In all cases, combining several buds in the same cure mechanically increases the number of potential interactions. It is preferable to start with a single macerate, evaluate its effects, and then add a second bud if needed, rather than putting together a complex blend from the outset without any experience of individual tolerance.
