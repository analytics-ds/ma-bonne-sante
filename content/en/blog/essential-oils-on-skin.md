Using **essential oils on skin** properly comes down to a few simple rules above all, dilution and dosage, since these plant extracts are among the most concentrated substances that can be applied to skin. Used well, they offer real benefits in natural skincare; used at the wrong dose, they can irritate or sensitize even healthy skin after just a few applications. This guide covers correct dilution, dosage by body area, the precautions to know, and which oils suit each skin type.

## Why dilute an essential oil before applying it to the skin

An essential oil is an aromatic extract most often obtained by distillation, with a concentration of active molecules far higher than that of a vegetable oil or a typical cosmetic active. This potency explains its effectiveness, but also the risk of irritation, redness, or allergic reaction when applied pure or at an excessive dose, particularly on skin as thin as that of the face.

Diluting an essential oil in a vegetable oil serves two purposes at once. It reduces the concentration of active molecules in direct contact with the skin, and it eases cutaneous absorption thanks to the fatty carrier. The choice of vegetable oil is not neutral: jojoba and hazelnut suit combination to oily skin, while sweet almond or calendula oil is gentler for dry or sensitive skin.

Some essential oils are described as dermocaustic, meaning they can burn or strongly irritate skin even when diluted at a low dose: this is the case for cinnamon, clove, oregano, or savory. Others, such as citrus essences (orange, lemon, bergamot), are photosensitizing and can cause pigmentation spots if the skin is exposed to sunlight after application. Always diluting therefore remains the baseline precaution, whichever essential oil is chosen and whichever area is treated, skin-related or not.

### The tolerance test in the crook of the elbow

Before using any new essential oil on the skin, a tolerance test is recommended, even for an oil considered well-tolerated. It involves placing a drop of the diluted mixture in the crook of the elbow or the wrist, then waiting 24 to 48 hours without washing the area.

The absence of redness, itching, or a burning sensation after that delay signals good tolerance for that specific essential oil. In case of a reaction, even a mild one, it is best to avoid that oil for skin use and turn to an alternative better suited to the skin.

## What dilution dosage by body area

The dosage of an essential oil changes noticeably depending on the body area concerned, since skin sensitivity is not the same everywhere. A dilution meant for a back massage, for instance, would be excessive if applied as is to the face.

| Application area | Generally recommended dilution | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Face | About 1% | Thinner skin, the most sensitive area |
| Body (excluding face) | Between 1 and 2% | Depending on tolerance and the oil chosen |
| Hair and scalp | About 1 to 2% | Diluted in an oil or a shampoo |
| Localized, occasional massage | Up to 5% | Short-term use, not daily |

These are general guidelines and do not replace the specific instructions for each essential oil, some of which call for even more cautious dilution due to their composition. The exact number of drops also depends on the container used: [essential oil dosage in a diffuser](/en/blog/how-many-drops-essential-oil-diffuser/) actually follows different logic, since it involves atmospheric diffusion rather than skin application.

In children under 6 and in babies, the dosages above generally do not apply: most essential oils are not recommended for skin use before this age, unless otherwise advised by a health professional trained in pediatric aromatherapy.

## How to apply essential oils to the face without irritating the skin

The face is the body area that tolerates insufficient dilution the least well, since the skin there is thinner and more vascularized than elsewhere. The lightest dilution from the table above therefore applies here as a priority, in a vegetable oil chosen according to skin type: jojoba for combination to oily skin, argan or avocado for dry skin, calendula for sensitive, reactive skin.

Application is generally done as a gentle massage with the fingertips, carefully avoiding the eye contour and mucous membranes. Some essential oils, such as citrus essences, are photosensitizing: it is best to reserve their facial application for an evening routine rather than before sun exposure.

For a targeted skin concern, such as recurring blemishes, one specific essential oil can complement an existing routine. The [guide to using tea tree essential oil on the face](/en/blog/tea-tree-oil-on-face/) covers this exact case, including dilution, frequency, and precautions.

Many commercial cosmetic products already contain one or more essential oils as active ingredients, at a regulated concentration well below that of a homemade blend. That is no reason to stack sources on the face without accounting for it: a cream already containing tea tree or lavender, combined with a homemade diluted blend of the same oil, can push past the skin's tolerance threshold without this being immediately noticeable.

## How to use essential oils on the body, in massage and in the bath

On the body, skin generally tolerates a somewhat stronger dilution than on the face, which leaves more room for a wellness massage or a targeted application on a specific area, such as the legs or back.

In massage, the dilution can go up to 5% for occasional use, provided the application is not repeated several times a day over a long period. A blend of several essential oils diluted in a vegetable oil remains the most common approach for massage, with each oil contributing its own property to the final mix.

In the bath, essential oils do not dissolve in water: they must first be mixed with a suitable dispersant (bath base, milk, or honey) before being poured into the water, otherwise they stay on the surface and can irritate the skin directly on contact. This precaution is often overlooked even though it determines the safety of aromatic bath use.

## Which essential oil to choose based on skin type or skin concern

Choosing an essential oil for the skin depends first on skin type and the targeted concern, well before the question of dosage. Some oils are known for their tolerance on sensitive skin, others for a targeted action on a specific concern.

**True lavender** and Roman chamomile suit sensitive, reactive skin, thanks to their well-documented soothing action. **Tea tree** instead targets blemish-prone skin and skin concerns of fungal or bacterial origin; the [complete tea tree protocol against fungal infections](/en/blog/tea-tree-essential-oil-fungal-infection/) covers this specific use in detail. Rose geranium suits combination to oily skin by regulating excess sebum, while rosewood and Italian helichrysum are more often sought for mature skin, notably to reduce the appearance of wrinkles and fine lines; the comparison of [essential oils for deep wrinkles](/en/blog/essential-oil-deep-wrinkles/) expands on this use case.

Some essential oils remain best avoided in everyday skin cosmetics, even when properly diluted: the dermocaustic oils mentioned above have no place in a skincare routine, whatever the skin type targeted. The choice therefore always comes down to cross-referencing skin type, the targeted concern, and the known tolerance of each essential oil.

Citrus essences such as sweet **orange** or **lemon** deserve a separate mention: valued in cosmetics for their scent and their brightening effect on dull skin, they remain photosensitizing and should therefore be reserved for evening application, never before sun exposure. This nuance comes up often in questions about choosing an essential oil for a given use, since cosmetic properties and precautions can vary considerably from one citrus essence to another within the same family.

## Precautions and contraindications for the skin

Certain populations and certain situations call for extra caution before any skin application of an essential oil, beyond the baseline dilution.

### Children and pregnant women

In **pregnant** and breastfeeding women, a large number of essential oils are contraindicated for skin application, particularly during the first trimester, due to compounds that may cross the skin barrier. Pharmacist or medical advice remains the best safeguard before any use in this situation.

In **children**, caution varies significantly by age: most essential oils are not recommended for skin application before age 3, and a smaller number remain allowed, heavily diluted, between ages 3 and 6. After age 6, dosages gradually move closer to adult levels, without ever fully matching them before adolescence.

On damaged or irritated skin, or on an open wound, applying an essential oil is generally discouraged, since the weakened skin barrier increases the risk of systemic absorption and irritation. People on medical treatment, particularly anticoagulant or hormonal therapy, should also seek advice before introducing a new essential oil into their skin routine.

People with asthma, epilepsy, or an allergy to plants in the same botanical family as a given essential oil (for example asteraceae for chamomile) should be extra cautious, including with skin application, since inhalation is unavoidable during the process. When in doubt, a pharmacist trained in aromatherapy remains the most accessible resource to confirm the choice of an essential oil and its dosage before regular skin use.

## What to do in case of a skin reaction or irritation

Despite all these precautions, a skin reaction remains possible, particularly on first use. The signs to watch for are redness, a burning or warm sensation, itching, or a localized rash at the application site.

In case of a reaction, the first step is to remove the essential oil from the skin using a neutral vegetable oil (water alone does not dissolve aromatic compounds and can even worsen the burning sensation by pushing them further into the skin). A gentle wash with a superfatted soap can follow once the excess oil has been removed.

If the reaction persists, spreads, or comes with swelling, difficulty breathing, or any other unusual sign, medical advice is needed without delay. In case of accidental contact with the eye, immediate rinsing with vegetable oil, never water, followed by medical advice if discomfort persists, is the reflex to remember.
