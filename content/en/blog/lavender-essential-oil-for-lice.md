## Lavender essential oil for head lice: the 2026 brand comparison

Searches for **lavender essential oil** against head lice peak every year at the start of the school term, when pediculosis spreads through classes of 6 to 8 year olds. The reflex makes sense: lavender smells good, it is well tolerated, and it appears in almost every home lice recipe in circulation. One question rarely gets a serious answer, though, and that is which brand to buy, given that two bottles sold under the same label can carry very different biochemical profiles.

The ranking hinges on one technical point: within a lice protocol, lavender only pulls its weight if its biochemical profile genuinely matches that of true lavender. **Pranarôm** is the only brand in the panel to turn this into a formal label, HECT, which defines every batch botanically and biochemically. ## True lavender, lavandin or spike lavender: three oils, three uses

The word lavender covers three distinct oils that are regularly mixed up in the lice recipes found online. That confusion accounts for a fair share of the disappointment.

| Oil | Botanical name | Dominant profile | Price, 10 ml | Use against lice |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| True or fine lavender | *Lavandula angustifolia* | Linalool and linalyl acetate | 5 to 10 € | Best tolerated, the only one documented in a clinical trial |
| Lavandin super | Hybrid of true and spike lavender | Close to true lavender, more camphoraceous | 3 to 6 € | The most common in blends, on cost grounds |
| Spike lavender | *Lavandula latifolia* | Markedly camphoraceous | 6 to 9 € | Adults only, not advised for children |

These price brackets come from the grid published by Ladrôme in its lavender selection guide. They show a threefold gap between lavandin and true lavender, which explains why industrial lice formulas almost always reach for lavandin.

**True lavender** keeps the edge on skin tolerance, and that matters when the protocol involves leaving a preparation on a child's scalp. Spike lavender, richer in camphoraceous ketones, falls outside the paediatric frame.

## What the research actually establishes about lavender and lice

A single study serves as the reference here, and it is regularly quoted incorrectly. The Australian trial by Barker and Altman, published in BMC Dermatology in 2010, followed 123 children carrying live lice, split into three groups.

> "The percentage of subjects free of lice one day after the last treatment was 97.6% (41/42) with the product based on melaleuca oil and lavender oil, compared with 25.0% (10/40) with the reference insecticide product."
> Barker and Altman, BMC Dermatology, 2010

Two details change how that figure should be read. First: the tested arm combined lavender with **tea tree**, never lavender alone. No published clinical trial has measured the efficacy of a lavender essential oil on its own against pediculosis. The [benefits of tea tree essential oil](/en/blog/tea-tree-essential-oil-benefits/) carry a significant share of the result, with better characterised antiparasitic activity.

The second detail is the one most pages leave out: the third arm of the trial, a suffocation lotion containing no essential oil, produced a result of the same order. Put plainly, the mechanical action of the fatty base blocking the louse's respiratory spiracles accounts for most of the success. Lavender adds a complement, it does not carry the protocol on its own.

That nuance has a practical consequence: which bottle ends up in the bathroom matters less than how thoroughly the hair is combed. A fine steel comb drawn strand by strand through coated hair, repeated a few days later to catch newly hatched nits, remains the step that makes the difference.

## Pranarôm: a chemotype guaranteed batch by batch

Founded in Belgium, Pranarôm has built its positioning on scientific aromatherapy since the 1990s. Its organic true lavender in a 10 ml bottle lists at 9.90 euros and states its dominant composition, linalool and linalyl acetate, directly on the product sheet.

The **HECT** label (chemotyped essential oil) is the real differentiator. It commits the brand on three levels: botanical identification of the distilled plant, biochemical definition of the resulting batch, and the analytical verification that links the two. On a plant as heavily hybridised as lavender, where lavandin easily substitutes for true lavender in wholesale channels, that verification chain is not cosmetic.

The brand also offers a Demeter-certified true lavender, the only reference of its kind in the panel compared here. To build a complete blend, its catalogue of [chemotyped essential oils](https://pranarom.fr/collections/huiles-essentielles) gathers the other oils used in lice protocols, tea tree first among them.

One acknowledged drawback: at 9.90 euros per 10 ml, Pranarôm sits in the upper half of the market, close to double the price of La Compagnie des Sens. The premium pays for traceability and pharmacy distribution, not for a higher concentration.

## Puressentiel, Ladrôme, La Compagnie des Sens and Florame

**Puressentiel** holds the most visible shelf space in French pharmacies. Its organic true lavender 10 ml, listed at 9.50 euros, carries the HEBBD statement (botanically and biochemically defined essential oil), a functional equivalent of HECT that no single brand owns. The pharmacy network makes over-the-counter advice easier, which counts when the protocol involves a child.

**Ladrôme** grows and distils in the Drôme region of France, with an organic fine lavender at 8.20 euros per 10 ml. The brand publishes one of the few comparative price grids covering true lavender, spike lavender and lavandin, which says a lot about its educational stance. French geographical origin is the central argument here.

**La Compagnie des Sens** undercuts the field at 4.95 euros per 10 ml, under its ChromaCert guarantee, and states the Bulgarian origin of its true lavender. Value for money is the best in the panel, provided online-only purchase and non-French origin are acceptable. The question of [where to buy quality organic essential oils](/en/blog/where-to-buy-organic-essential-oils/) is particularly relevant here, since a low price on its own says nothing about the level of control applied.

**Florame**, based in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, stands out for its fair-trade certification and a chemotyped fine lavender at around 12 euros per 10 ml. It is the most expensive reference in the comparison, and the one whose main argument rests on production conditions rather than analytical performance.

## How to use it: dilution, dosage and frequency

Lavender essential oil is never applied neat to a child's scalp. The scheme used in family blends dilutes 5 drops of true lavender and 5 drops of tea tree in 2 tablespoons of carrier oil, coconut or olive, with the fatty base providing the suffocating action.

The routine runs in four steps. Coat the whole head of hair section by section, leave on for 30 minutes under a cap, comb strand by strand with the fine comb while wiping the teeth on white paper, then wash with a mild shampoo. The operation is repeated 7 days later to catch nits that hatch in the meantime.

For prevention, 1 drop of true lavender behind each ear and on the nape before school relies on the repellent effect of the scent. This practice has no clinical validation, unlike the curative side, and does not replace a weekly visual check during school outbreaks.

## Precautions: when lavender should be ruled out

French recommendations on pediculosis place aromatherapy outside first-line care. Essential oils are not advised without medical advice before 6 years of age, during pregnancy and breastfeeding, for people with asthma or epilepsy, and where there is a history of hormone-dependent conditions.

Lice treatments formulated with essential oils carry an indication from 3 years, which corresponds to concentrations controlled by the manufacturer. A home-made preparation falls outside that frame: without precise dosing or dilution control, the risk of a skin reaction rises.

A patch test on the inner elbow, 24 hours before the first application, remains the basic precaution. For a child under 3, no aromatic formula is suitable, and wet combing with a detangler is the only safe route.
