## Why natural lice products work by suffocation rather than neurotoxicity

The market's shift towards insecticide-free formulas is not a passing trend. It rests on an entomological fact: head lice develop resistance to neurotoxic insecticides and pass it on to their offspring, which has steadily eroded the efficacy of products that worked very well twenty years ago.

Mechanical products sidestep the problem entirely. Their principle is physical: a fatty substance or an occlusive film blocks the spiracles, the breathing openings along the louse's flanks, and the insect suffocates. No resistance can develop against suffocation, because there is neither a nerve target nor an enzyme to mutate. That is why families of products as different as a plant-oil lotion and a synthetic silicone end up sharing the same mechanism.

### What natural really covers on a lice product

The most common confusion on the shelf concerns dimeticone. It is regularly listed among natural treatments because it contains no insecticide, which is accurate. But dimeticone is a synthetic silicone polymer with no plant origin whatsoever. A product can therefore be insecticide-free, very well tolerated, clinically documented, and still not be natural.

The two boxes to tick on the label are separate: no neurotoxic insecticide on one side, plant origin of the ingredients on the other. The Pranarôm and Puressentiel ranges tick both, claiming a 100% natural formula. Dimeticone lotions only tick the first.

## Dimeticone lotions, effective but not natural

This is the best-documented family on the shelf, and the one that raises the most questions of vocabulary. Dimeticone forms a silicone film that blocks the louse's airways, through a purely physical mechanism with no action on the nervous system.

> In a randomised equivalence trial published in the BMJ in 2005, a 4% dimeticone lotion cleared head lice in 70% of treated participants, compared with 75% for a reference insecticide lotion, a difference that was not statistically significant.
> Burgess IF, Brown CM, Lee PN, *BMJ*, 2005

That result explains dimeticone's place in first-line recommendations in France. It also explains why it appears so often in online lists of natural treatments, through a slide between insecticide-free and natural. For a family specifically looking for a plant-based formula, the distinction is decisive, and it only shows up in the ingredient list.

## The nit comb, the step no product replaces

No lotion, whatever its formula, dissolves the substance that glues the nit to the hair shaft. Removal is mechanical, and it calls for a close-toothed steel comb passed strand by strand through damp hair. It is the longest step of the protocol, the most thankless, and the one behind most failures wrongly blamed on the product.

| Step | Action | Indicative duration |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Application | Coat hair and scalp, roots included | 5 minutes |
| 2. Contact time | Follow the time stated on the leaflet, without cutting it short | 5 to 15 minutes |
| 3. Combing | Fine steel comb, strand by strand, wiped on white paper after each pass | 30 to 45 minutes depending on hair length |
| 4. Rinsing and washing | Mild shampoo, then comb again through damp hair | 10 minutes |
| 5. Second application | Repeat after 7 days to reach lice hatched from nits since | Full protocol |

The second application after seven days is not a commercial precaution. Nits already present at the first treatment hatch in the following days, and a protocol stopped after a single application lets the infestation restart with a fresh generation.

## Coconut oil, vinegar and baking soda: what homemade preparations are worth

Coconut and olive oil applied as a mask work on the same suffocation principle as commercial lotions, with one significant reservation: coverage is uneven, contact time is approximate, and no efficacy data has been published on these practices. For under 5 €, they are an honest stopgap between two treatments, not a treatment in their own right.

White vinegar holds a place of its own in advice passed down in families. It has no effect on a live louse, but its acidity helps loosen nits before combing, which makes it a combing aid rather than a lice product. Baking soda has no data at all to support use on the scalp against head lice.

Two practices circulate and deserve to be ruled out plainly: applying undiluted essential oils to the scalp, which risks irritation with no gain in efficacy, and using products not intended for human skin, which have no place on a child's head. A lotion with a short contact time cuts the negotiation short, with 5 minutes against 10 to 15 minutes for competing formulas. The lotion and shampoo duo at 29.90 € covers both treatment and washing, and the comb included in the two-lotion duo avoids a separate purchase.

## The mistakes that make a natural lice treatment fail

- **Treating only once.** Without the second application after seven days, nits hatched in the meantime restart the infestation, and the product takes the blame.
- **Cutting the combing short.** Thirty minutes of fine combing on long hair cannot be compressed into five, whatever product was applied beforehand.
- **Treating the whole family as a precaution.** Only people actually carrying lice should be treated, after checking with a comb on damp hair. Systematic preventive treatment has no basis.
- **Forgetting the immediate environment.** Hats, scarves, pillowcases and car seats should be washed at 60 °C or sealed in a bag for 48 hours, since lice do not survive long away from a scalp.
