Waking up several times a night without being able to fall back asleep easily leads many people to ask **which plant to choose against nighttime waking**. Unlike plants that mainly target falling asleep, some species act more on staying asleep once the night is underway, which changes the choice to make.

## Why do we wake up several times a night?

An isolated nighttime waking is nothing unusual: sleep naturally alternates between light, deep, and REM cycles, and a micro-waking occurs at each transition. The trouble starts when these wakings repeat and falling back asleep becomes difficult, often under the effect of stress, an overheated bedroom, or a dinner eaten too late.

The nervous system plays a central role in this phenomenon: nervous activity still elevated at bedtime weakens deep sleep and makes each cycle transition more likely to turn into a full waking. This is precisely where sleep plants come in, calming the nervous system before and during the night rather than forcing sleep the way some medications do.

Several factors encourage the onset or worsening of nighttime waking. Caffeine and alcohol consumed in the evening disrupt sleep structure for hours after they are absorbed, even when they do not prevent falling asleep itself. A bedroom that is too warm or too bright keeps the body in a state of light alertness that makes every micro-waking easier to trigger. Age also plays a known role: deep sleep naturally declines over the years, which makes waking more frequent and harder to ignore without necessarily being a disorder in itself.

## Valerian, the reference plant against nighttime waking

**Valerian** remains the most studied plant for sleep disorders, waking included. Its valerenic acids act on the same receptors as many sleeping pills, with a more moderate intensity, which reduces the time it takes to fall asleep while promoting more continuous sleep through the night.

Taken alone, valerian mostly targets the falling-asleep phase. It is in combination with hops that it becomes particularly relevant for nighttime waking, a pairing detailed further below.

It is most often taken 30 to 60 minutes before bedtime, as a tea, capsule, or dry extract, with an effect sometimes considered faster than other plants on this list. Its strong taste, often described as earthy, explains why many people prefer a capsule form over an infusion.

## Passionflower and California poppy to calm the nervous system at night

When nighttime waking comes with racing thoughts, **passionflower** helps relax the nervous system and limit the mental agitation that prevents falling back asleep. It carries no known risk of dependence, which allows for regular use in adults.

**California poppy** (Eschscholzia californica) pairs well with passionflower: the two plants are often mentioned together against nighttime or early waking, with a calming effect on the central nervous system rather than a blunt sedative effect. This combination is particularly suited to waking followed by rumination that is hard to stop.

Unlike opium poppy, California poppy carries no risk of dependence or tolerance at the doses typically used for sleep. It is generally taken as a tincture or capsule in the evening, sometimes combined with passionflower in the same formula to reinforce the calming effect on waking accompanied by marked mental agitation.

## Hawthorn, hops, and linden for deep sleep

Three lesser-known plants specifically target staying asleep rather than falling asleep alone.

**Hawthorn** is traditionally used to calm palpitations and cardiac nervousness, two factors that can trigger an abrupt waking in the middle of the night. **Hops**, in synergy with valerian, are recognized for limiting both the time it takes to fall asleep and the frequency of nighttime waking, two complementary effects that each plant taken alone covers less well. **Linden**, gentler, suits nights that are simply restless, without a marked disorder.

### Lemon balm as an evening tea

**Lemon balm** finds its place in this trio in cases of digestive spasms associated with nighttime waking, a frequent but often overlooked cause. Taken as an infusion before bedtime, it reduces the stress and nervousness that keep the waking-rumination-difficulty-falling-back-asleep cycle going.

## Magnesium and melatonin, useful complements

Alongside plants, two nutrients come up regularly in managing nighttime waking. **Magnesium** helps relax the nervous system and muscles, a role close to that of sedative plants but through a different mechanism, which allows it to be combined safely. **Melatonin**, the body's natural sleep hormone, mainly helps reset a shifted sleep-wake cycle, rather than treat an isolated waking episode.

These two options are most often available as tablets or capsules, a practical alternative for people who do not enjoy the taste of herbal teas. The [comparison of natural sleep supplements](/en/blog/natural-sleep-supplement-brands/) details formulas that combine plants, magnesium, and melatonin.

Magnesium occurs naturally in certain foods (nuts, legumes, dark chocolate), but a typical diet does not always provide enough to cover needs, which justifies a short course in case of frequent nighttime waking. Melatonin, for its part, is generally taken at a low dose, about one to two hours before the desired bedtime, to support a gradual shift of the sleep-wake cycle rather than to force immediate sleep.

## How to use these plants against nighttime waking

The choice of form influences both effectiveness and ease of daily use.

| Form | Speed of action | Suited to |
|---|---|---|
| Tea / infusion | Moderate | Regular evening ritual |
| Capsule or tablet | Moderate to fast | Taste hard to tolerate as a tea |
| Mother tincture | Fast | Occasional need, precise dosing |
| Combined supplement | Varies by formula | Waking linked to a shifted cycle |

**Tea** remains the most accessible form: the [comparison of teas for sleep](/en/blog/best-tea-for-sleep/) details the steeping times specific to each plant mentioned here. For a more occasional need, mother tincture offers faster absorption, provided the dosage indicated on the bottle is respected.

Dosages vary noticeably from one plant to another. Valerian is most often used between 300 and 600 mg of dry extract, passionflower around 300 to 500 mg, and hawthorn between 200 and 500 mg per dose. Hops, rarely taken alone, usually complement a valerian-based formula rather than being dosed on their own. These figures remain indicative: the exact dosage depends on the concentration of the chosen product and should be checked on the label or with a pharmacist.

### Precautions and contraindications to know

These plants remain generally well tolerated, but some precautions apply. Passionflower and California poppy are not recommended during pregnancy due to a possible effect on uterine tone. Valerian and hops can strengthen the effect of sedative or anxiolytic treatments already prescribed, which calls for pharmacist advice when a treatment is ongoing. In children, linden and chamomile are generally preferred over more active plants such as passionflower.

Unlike many hypnotic medications, these plants are not associated with a known risk of dependence, which does not remove the benefit of favoring courses of a few weeks rather than continuous, uninterrupted use.

## When to see a healthcare professional

Plants against nighttime waking reach their limits when facing certain causes that go beyond simple, passing discomfort. Waking accompanied by loud snoring and breathing pauses suggests sleep apnea, which requires a medical diagnosis. Likewise, waking systematically followed by night sweats, a frequent need to urinate, or marked anxiety upon waking deserves to be discussed with a healthcare professional rather than treated with plants alone.

Sudden nighttime waking appearing after several years of stable sleep, or coming with unexplained weight loss, daytime palpitations, or repeated hot flashes, also warrants medical advice: these symptoms can reflect a hormonal imbalance rather than simple sleep fragility. In this case, the plants covered here can support medical care, but do not replace it.
